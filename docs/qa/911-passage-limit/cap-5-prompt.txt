=== RECENT NARRATIVE ===
You do not answer Calyx with another argument.

You turn the bronze token once in your palm, feel its stamped number through the ache your fist has made, and put your thumb briefly to your temple—not as forgiveness, not as a claim of good faith large enough to mend anything. Only an acknowledgment that you heard the boundary.

“I’ll give it quiet,” you say.

Calyx inclines her head. She does not thank you.

Tam keeps both hands against the memorial table. Their folder remains closed beneath Calyx’s hand. Elian’s brass key is a small hard shape in the dark of her coat, and she does not look at you as you pass. Ivo remains by the lantern rack, facing the clock and whatever waits inside the hours before dawn. Ren’s wafer stays in his hand, dark as a shut eye.

Then the arcade door shuts behind you, and the hall’s rain-muted silence belongs to the people you have left in it.

Outside, the weather lattice has made the loading arcade into a corridor of silver ropes. Freight lifts slide through their shafts behind ribbed safety glass; each descent trembles faintly in the soles of your shoes. Beyond a row of chained pallet sleds, a narrow door bears the Wickglass mark in blue enamel: three transit lines around a falling drop.

The Guild-lit room is less a room than a glass-fronted dispatch box raised over the freight floor. Its lamps are old-spectrum amber, deliberately stupid, incapable of pretending they have not seen what they see. Route slates hang blank on one wall. A kettle works itself toward a boil on a hotplate. No recording lens faces the table.

Kessa Brin sits beneath the lamps with her jacket hung over the chair beside her. She has a chipped ceramic cup between both hands. At your entrance, she checks the token, then the clock over the door.

“Before rotation,” she says. “Good.”

She does not ask you to sit. She slides a folded sheet across the table instead. It is paper, Guild stock, its watermark visible where the lamplight catches it.

At the top is a name.

**Nera Voss.**

Below it, in a hand older than the print: **continuity harmonized following a coolant-line casualty.**

The phrase sits there with all the clean cruelty Vesper gives its dead.

Kessa watches you read it.

“Four years ago, your office reviewed the casualty file,” she says. “The contractor who ruptured the line had a Helix indemnity. The indemnity did not cover a courier who opened the wrong emergency shutter because the station map had been reconciled after a fire.”

Rain skates down the glass between you and the lifts. Somewhere below, a coupling clangs shut.

“Nera’s wife got a sealed settlement and a revised memory of why Nera never came home. Wickglass got an instruction not to contest the civic finding.” Kessa taps the bottom of the sheet with one blunt finger. “Your name is on the review notation. Not the harm. The notation.”

You remember nothing at first. Then perhaps that is the point: a gray-sealed queue; forms feeding one another; a signature made because three offices had already agreed that the line was administratively closed.

“The obligation isn’t money,” Kessa says. “It is one sentence, in your own hand, for our dead-book. You write that you reviewed a record you did not independently verify, and that your review helped close a death into something the surviving person could not contest.”

Her gaze does not soften. It does not need to.

“You may refuse. The Guild will keep the token open. But not forever, and not without consequence to the people who still treat your seal as a warning.”

A freight horn gives one low note through the floor.

Kessa reaches into her satchel and produces a second paper: a narrow dispatch receipt, blank except for a date, a time, and a single handwritten line.

**A municipal adjunct asked last week which Wickglass carriers could be isolated through family obligations. No names were provided. No list was given. We declined the question.**

“I can put that in your hand after you write,” she says. “It is not proof against Aster or Sol. It is not a route, a carrier, or an issuer. It is what the Guild was asked, and what the Guild refused to sell.”

The dead-book sheet waits beside the blank receipt. Back in Memorial Hall, Tam’s folder remains closed because Tam chose it closed—not because anyone has solved what safety costs.

Kessa folds her hands around her cooling cup.

“So,” she says. “Do you sign the sentence?”

I step to the dispatch-box doorway and catch sight of Niko Rell on the loading arcade. I raise an open hand rather than call across the floor. "Niko. Can we talk here a moment? I want to hear what you have to say."
Your fist closes.

For one sharp second, the bronze disk is everything you have been trying to make the night become: a fact so hard it can be kept and destroyed in the same motion; a woman who can be summoned by saying the wrong name loudly enough; a witness whose absence can be repaired by demanding it.

Then the metal does not yield.

Its rim bites deeper into your palm. Your thumb finds the stamped number. The token remains whole, cold, and indisputably Kessa Brin’s—not Ressa Morn’s. Ressa is elsewhere in the station, with Ivo’s unfinished accounting waiting for him at dawn. Kessa is somewhere beyond the rain-striped panes, under Guild lamps, expecting the holder of this particular token for one private conversation before the freight rotation turns.

“Kessa Brin is Ressa Morn,” you say.

No one answers at once. The claim makes a small, ugly vacancy in the hall.

Ren lifts his eyes from the wafer. “No,” he says. “That is not supported by anything before us, and I will not record it as identification.”

Ivo’s shoulders draw tight. He does not defend Ressa; he does not need to. “Ressa wouldn’t send a courier to be herself,” he says, low. “She’d come. Or she’d tell you why she wouldn’t.”

Calyx’s hand remains on Tam’s closed folder. “The Guild’s message is bounded,” she says. “Do not enlarge it into a different person because the actual obligation is inconvenient.”

Rain rattles the high glass like loose type in an old machine.

“Ora Pell,” you call toward the arcade. “Witness this.”

The name travels no farther than the lanterns. It catches in wet wool, in brass plaques, in the mild bitter steam rising from abandoned tea. Ora does not appear. She is not in this room, and no one here has the right to draw her back across the station merely because a record would feel steadier with her standing beside it.
